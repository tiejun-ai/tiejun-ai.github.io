    Modeling Long User Histories for Recommendation: Classic Approaches and Douyin’s Cross-Attention

    October 31, 2026

    How Douyin makes short-video ranking affordable with user histories of up to 10K interactions: stacked target-to-history cross-attention, request-level sharing, and stochastic-length training that averages 2K interactions per sequence. I compare the design with DIN, SIM, BST, and HSTU, and share my thoughts on its tradeoffs and implications for scaling recommendation.

    Paper: Make It Long, Keep It Fast: End-to-End 10K Long User Behavior Sequence Modeling for Billion-Scale Douyin Recommendation (Guan et al., ByteDance, WWW 2026)

    This post follows arXiv v3, revised May 19, 2026. The PDF uses the shorter title “Make It Long, Keep It Fast: End-to-End 10k-Sequence Modeling at Billion Scale on Douyin Recommendation.”

    TL;DR#

    Modern recommendation systems use user behavior sequences to predict how users will respond to candidate items. Longer sequences provide richer preference signals but increase training and inference costs. This paper makes user histories of up to 10K interactions practical in Douyin’s ranking system through three techniques:

    • Linear-cost cross-attention: Stacked Target-to-History Cross Attention (STCA) replaces full user history self-attention with stacked candidate-to-history cross-attention, reducing attention complexity from O(L²) to O(L), where L is user history length.
    • Shared user history encoding: Request Level Batching (RLB) transfers and encodes each request’s user history once, then reuses it across multiple ranking candidates.
    • Shorter training sequences: Stochastic-length training averages 2K interactions per sequence, includes occasional histories up to 10K, and supports 10K at inference.

    In a one-month A/B test, the complete system improved Douyin’s Finish metric by 3.35% and App Stay Time by 0.93%, with an estimated 17% increase in total inference cost. (Sections 3–4)

    Problem: Model User History in a Recommendation Ranking Model#

    Given a user history sequence, the ranking model predicts how a user will respond to a candidate video:

    Input:  user history H = [(video, action), ...] and candidate video t
Output: P(finish t | H), plus other engagement predictions

    In the Douyin paper, a token is one historical interaction, with video and action features; 10K tokens means 10,000 interactions. This is a candidate-scoring model within a recommender’s ranking stage.

    How to model user history in the ranking model? The following classic approaches make different tradeoffs when using that history.

    DIN: Target-Aware Pooling of User History#

    DIN: Deep Interest Network (Alibaba, KDD 2018) weights each historical behavior by its relevance to the candidate, then pools the weighted embeddings for click prediction:

    u(t) = Σᵢ a(hᵢ, t) · hᵢ

    Here, hᵢ is a historical behavior embedding, t is the candidate embedding, and a(hᵢ, t) is a relevance weight produced by a learned feedforward network. DIN uses unnormalized weights to preserve interest intensity.

    This candidate-aware pooling is already linear in history length, but does not explicitly model event order or history–history dependencies. STCA builds on target-aware attention with stacked, multi-head history reads.

    SIM: Target-Aware Retrieval from User History#

    SIM: Search-based User Interest Modeling (Alibaba, CIKM 2020) uses a General Search Unit (GSU) to retrieve candidate-relevant behaviors from the user’s history, then an Exact Search Unit (ESU) to predict clicks from that subset.

    Example: For a hiking-video candidate, the GSU might narrow user history [cooking, hiking, comedy, camping] to [hiking, camping]. The ESU then uses the selected behaviors and the candidate to predict click probability.

    This reduces computation, but retrieval can exclude useful historical evidence.

    Model interactions across the sequence#

    BST: Behavior Sequence Transformer for E-commerce Recommendation in Alibaba (DLP-KDD 2019) adds Transformer self-attention to a ranking model. It embeds the user behavior sequence together with the target item, adds time-based positional features, and applies self-attention over the sequence. The resulting representation is combined with other features and passed to an MLP for click prediction. Compared with DIN’s candidate-conditioned pooling, BST applies self-attention across the history and target item, explicitly modeling history–history interactions.

    BST architecture. Figure 1 from Chen et al., 2019.

    HSTU (Meta, ICML 2024) also models interactions across sequence positions in its sequential-transduction formulation of recommendation, with causal masking and attention designed for user actions. See my HSTU explanation for the full architecture.

    Conceptual diagram created for this blog, based on the causal ranking formulation in HSTU. Actual item/action tokenization and attention operations vary by model; the matrix illustrates allowed connections.

    At fixed model width and depth, dense attention over a full sequence has O(L²) computational complexity. HSTU improves practical efficiency with specialized kernels, stochastic-length training, and reuse across candidates; stochastic-length training can reduce expected training complexity. (HSTU, Section 3.2)

    The challenge is to increase model capacity and history length within training and serving budgets. STCA builds on candidate-aware attention with stacked history reads, while request-level sharing and stochastic-length training make longer histories affordable.

    Method Explained#

    STCA: Repeatedly Querying User History with the Candidate#

    Figure 2 from Guan et al., 2026. Panel (A) shows STCA; panels (B) and (C) show request-level batching and extrapolation-aware training.

    Given a history of length L, full self-attention produces roughly L × L attention weights per head. STCA instead uses one candidate query, producing 1 × L weights per head at each layer. At fixed model width and depth, its attention cost is linear in L. The pseudocode below uses M stacked STCA layers:

    x_t = embed_target(t)
X = embed_history(H)  # video + action + position embeddings
q₁ = LayerNorm(SwiGLUFFN_1(x_t))
summaries = []

for i in 1..M:
    Xᵢ = LayerNorm(SwiGLUFFN_i(X))  # applied independently to each token
    oᵢ = multi_head_cross_attention(qᵢ, Xᵢ)
    summaries.append(oᵢ)
    if i < M:
        qᵢ₊₁ = fuse(x_t, summaries)

z = SwiGLUFFN(concat(summaries + [x_t]) @ W_Z)
h = RankMixer(z, other_user_and_candidate_features)
prediction = sigmoid(linear(h))

    Conceptual pseudocode; attention projections and query-fusion details are omitted. Each layer transforms the original history embeddings X with its own SwiGLUFFN_i, as in Equation 2.

    User history embeddings X combine video, action, and position features and are transformed independently by SwiGLU and LayerNorm. The same operations turn the candidate embedding x_t into the initial query: q₁ = LayerNorm(SwiGLUFFN(x_t)). (Equation 3)

    SwiGLU uses two learned projections: one produces features, while the other applies SiLU (also called Swish with β = 1), defined as SiLU(z) = z · sigmoid(z), to gate those features:

    SwiGLUFFN(x) = ((xWᵤ) ⊙ (xWᵥ ⊙ sigmoid(xWᵥ)))Wₒ

    Here, ⊙ means elementwise multiplication. The learned gate modulates each feature before the output projection. (Equation 1)

    Each layer transforms X into Xᵢ and uses cross-attention with qᵢ to produce oᵢ. To form the next query, fusion concatenates x_t and all accumulated summaries, then applies a learned projection, SwiGLUFFN, and LayerNorm. (Equation 7)

    To generate z, the final target-aware history summary, concatenate all layer summaries with the candidate embedding x_t, apply the learned projection W_Z, then apply SwiGLUFFN. (Equation 8)

    RankMixer: Scaling Up Ranking Models in Industrial Recommenders (Zhu et al., ByteDance, 2025) combines STCA’s history summary z with other user and candidate features. It uses token mixing to exchange information across feature tokens and per-token feedforward networks to learn their interactions, then feeds the result to an engagement prediction head.

    Each layer refines the candidate’s query, while history tokens are transformed independently. This enables repeated history reads without introducing history–history attention.

    RLB (Request Level Batching): Share a Request’s User History#

    Even linear-cost cross-attention can be expensive if the same long history is copied and encoded for every training example.

    For eight labeled candidates from the same request, pointwise inference can repeat the same history encoding eight times. RLB groups them so the history is transferred and encoded once.

    Diagram created for this blog, based on Section 3.2 of the paper.

    RLB shares the history embedding lookups and the history-side SwiGLUFFN and LayerNorm calculations in every STCA layer:

    X = embed_history(H)  # once per request
for i in 1..M:
    Xᵢ = LayerNorm(SwiGLUFFN_i(X))  # once per layer; reused by all candidates

    These calculations depend only on the shared history, so all candidates reuse the same encoded history matrices Xᵢ. The history payload is also serialized and transferred from CPU to GPU once per request. (Sections 3.1.1 and 3.2)

    RLB alone improves end-to-end training throughput by 2.2×. (Section 4.3)

    Train on variable lengths, serve long histories#

    Training every example with 10K interactions is still costly. The method instead randomly reduces user history length in training while keeping the average around 2K.

    Schematic created for this blog, based on Section 3.3 and Appendix A.4 of the paper. The illustrated lengths are examples, not measured samples.

    Three details make this work:

    • Sample the length with an asymmetric, U-shaped Beta distribution. Its parameters favor many short windows and occasional long windows while controlling the average.
    • Keep the most recent interactions for that length. In the reported experiment, this temporal suffix selection beats random selection from the history.
    • Balance and pack the batch. A token budget and a ragged attention kernel reduce padding and uneven workloads.

    The ablation study shows that including occasional long training sequences improves performance on long inference histories, while increasing the average training length yields diminishing returns. This supports mixing many short sequences with occasional long ones to balance accuracy and training cost. (Section 4.4, Tables 3–4)

    Experiments and Results#

    Scaling Law: Longer Histories and Larger Models#

    The paper reports scaling-law behavior in recommendation: ranking quality improves consistently as user history length and STCA model capacity increase. Figure 1 examines both dimensions:

    Figure 1 from Guan et al., 2026. Finish-AUC lift is measured in percentage points; parameter counts refer to the STCA sequence module.

    • Scale the history: At fixed model capacity, longer histories improve ranking quality by exposing more user behavior.
    • Scale the model: At comparable history lengths, larger STCA modules achieve higher quality, suggesting that additional capacity helps extract more useful preference signals.

    These results highlight the importance of modeling long user histories to improve recommendation quality: longer user histories provide useful preference signals that shorter histories miss. They also support history length as a scaling axis alongside model capacity over the evaluated configurations. STCA, RLB, and stochastic-length training make this joint scaling practical within production resource budgets.

    My Thoughts#

    The strongest idea: spending computation where it matters#

    I find the choice of where to spend computation most compelling. Target-aware cross-attention reduces attention cost from O(L²) to O(L), freeing the budget to process longer histories. Target-aware attention already exists in DIN; what stands out here is combining stacked history reads with training and systems techniques that make long-history modeling practical and powerful in production.

    Request-level optimization#

    RLB offers a useful engineering lesson: when many candidates share the same user history and context, reusing that computation should be a core engineering consideration. Grouping work at the request level can reduce repeated encoding and data movement. Meta’s HSTU follows a related principle through M-FALCON, which batches candidates and reuses history computation during inference.

    Long user histories#

    Most importantly, the results provide empirical evidence of scaling-law behavior as user history length and model capacity increase. In my view, this makes long user histories a promising scaling dimension for recommendation. I expect further progress in architectures and training systems that incorporate long user histories efficiently to improve recommendation quality.
